Define it first

What is Public Administration?

In plain words

Public administration is the work of putting government decisions into action: turning laws, policies and budgets into real services that people use every day.

The term has two meanings: (1) the activity done by government offices and employees, and (2) the field of study that teaches how to do that work well. This report uses both.

Think of it like this

Congress and the local councils write the recipe (the law). Public administration is the kitchen that cooks the meal and serves it to the people.

Examples you already know

  • DPWH building a flood-control dike
  • LTO issuing your driver's license
  • A barangay health worker giving vaccines
  • DepEd hiring teachers and buying textbooks
Formal definition

"Public administration is detailed and systematic execution of public law." Woodrow Wilson, 1887

"All those operations having for their purpose the fulfillment or enforcement of public policy." Leonard D. White, 1926

"The art and science of management as applied to affairs of state." Dwight Waldo, 1955

Define it first

Is it the same as running a company?

Both plan, budget and manage people. But they differ in purpose and rules.

QuestionPublic administration (government)Private administration (business)
Main goalServe the public interest, even when it earns nothingEarn profit for the owners
Answers toCitizens, through elected officials, COA, the Ombudsman and the courtsOwners and shareholders
Money comes fromTaxes, fees and loans approved in a public budgetSales, investors and loans
RulesCan only do what the law allows; strict rules on hiring and buyingCan do anything the law does not forbid
CustomersEveryone; a public hospital cannot refuse a patient for being poorCan choose its market and its customers

Why this matters: same management tools, different purpose and rules. That is why public administration is studied as its own field.

Words to know

Four words you will hear all day

Government

The institutions with legal authority to make and enforce rules: the President and agencies, Congress, the courts, and local government units (LGUs).

ExampleYour city hall and its Sangguniang Panlungsod.

Governance

How a society is steered: how decisions are made and carried out, by government plus business and citizens' groups. UNDP (1997): "the exercise of economic, political and administrative authority to manage a country's affairs at all levels."

ExampleA city deciding a flood plan together with barangays, NGOs and businesses.

Bureaucracy

The organized system of government offices and workers, with ranks, written rules and records (Max Weber's model). It is not bad by itself; it becomes "red tape" when rules slow people down.

ExampleA department with bureaus, divisions and sections.

Public policy

A course of action government chooses to deal with a public problem. Thomas Dye (1972): "whatever governments choose to do or not to do."

ExampleFree tuition in state universities and colleges (RA 10931).

Easy way to remember: government is the "who". Governance is the "how", and today many players take part in it.

How Public Administration was born

Backstory · Roots of the field

Where Public Administration came from

Step 1 · What was happening · 1800s

The "spoils system"

  • After each election, winners handed government jobs to their supporters: "to the victor belong the spoils" (Sen. William Marcy, 1832).
  • Officials were untrained and changed every election.
Step 2 · The problem people saw · 1881

A president is killed

  • U.S. President James Garfield was shot by a disappointed job-seeker who believed he was owed a post.
  • The public demanded an end to jobs-for-supporters.
Step 3 · The idea that came out · 1883–1920s

A science of administration

  • 1883 Pendleton Act: hire by merit exams.
  • 1887 Woodrow Wilson: study administration apart from politics.
  • 1911 Taylor's scientific management; Weber's model of bureaucracy.
So the conclusion was

Running government is a skill to be learned, not a prize to be given away. That idea gave birth to the field of Public Administration.

Why the old model broke down

Backstory · 1945 – 2000s

Why the old model stopped working

Step 1 · What was happening · 1945–1970s

Big government

  • After WWII, governments grew fast: welfare programs, state firms, large bureaucracies.
  • People expected government to do everything by itself.
Step 2 · The problem people saw · 1970s–80s

The money ran out

  • Oil shocks (1973, 1979) caused "stagflation": rising prices and joblessness.
  • Deficits and debts ballooned; bureaucracy was blamed as slow and wasteful.
  • The 1980s debt crisis hit developing countries, the Philippines included.
Step 3 · The idea that came out · 1979–1990s

New Public Management

  • Thatcher (UK, 1979), Reagan (US, 1981), New Zealand (from 1984).
  • Privatize, contract out, measure results, treat citizens as customers.
So the conclusion was

Each new model was invented to fix the problems of the one before it. Our three topics are answers to those problems.

Big picture

Four ways of thinking about government

A field is "emerging" when it became important only recently, because older ways of governing were no longer enough. Here is how thinking changed:

1880s – 1970s

Traditional PA

The idea

Government should be run by trained, neutral experts who follow clear rules, kept separate from politics.

In practice

Civil service exams, offices arranged by rank, everything written down.

1980s – 1990s

New Public Management

The idea

Government is slow and costly, so it should copy business: cut costs, compete, and measure results.

In practice

Selling state firms, hiring private contractors, performance targets.

2000s onward

New Public Governance

The idea

No single office can solve big problems, so government should work with business and civil society as partners.

In practice

Public-private partnerships; NGOs sitting in local councils.

2005 onward

Digital-Era Governance

The idea

Use technology to put scattered offices back together and serve people through one door.

In practice

One app for many services (eGovPH); shared databases.

A · Public Enterprise Management

What it is — and why it exists

1The problemPrivate firms go where there is money to be made. A far barangay buys too little, so no company builds there.
2What that is calledEconomists call this a market failure: the market alone leaves some people unserved.
3The answerGovernment runs the business itself through a GOCC. Managing those corporations well — and bringing the profits back — is public enterprise management.
Define it first

First, what is a corporation?

In plain words

A corporation is an organization that the law treats like a "person": it can own property, sign contracts, borrow money, and sue or be sued in its own name, separate from its owners.

Owners hold shares (stock). A board of directors sets the direction, and officers such as the president or CEO run the daily work.

Think of it like this

A family sari-sari store is not a corporation: if the owner dies, the store may close. A big listed company is one: it keeps going even when its owners change.

Two kinds of corporations

  • Stock corporation: has shares and can pay part of its profit (dividends) to its owners. Example: Land Bank of the Philippines.
  • Non-stock corporation: has no shares; it does not distribute profit and is used for service or charity. Example: a school foundation.
Formal definition

"A corporation is an artificial being created by operation of law, having the right of succession and the powers, attributes, and properties expressly authorized by law or incidental to its existence." Revised Corporation Code (RA 11232), Sec. 2

Define it first

So what is a GOCC?

In plain words

A GOCC is a corporation that the government owns (fully, or more than half of its shares) and that serves a public need. It looks like a business but works for the public.

Most are created by a special law called a charter. Worldwide, the general term is public enterprise or state-owned enterprise; GOCC is the Philippine legal name.

Think of it like this

A GOCC is "government in business clothes": it has a board, earns income and owns assets like a company, but its owner is the State and its boss is the public interest.

Three kinds under RA 10149

  • GOCC: a regular government corporation. e.g., PAGCOR, PCSO
  • GFI (government financial institution): a bank or fund handling public money. e.g., Landbank, DBP
  • GICP: an agency given some corporate powers and its own funds. e.g., MIAA, PPA, PDIC (named in the law)
Formal definition

"Any agency organized as a stock or non-stock corporation, vested with functions relating to public needs ... and owned by the Government ... either wholly or ... to the extent of at least a majority of its outstanding capital stock." RA 10149, Sec. 3(o)

Words to know

Words to know: when the market falls short

The market

Buyers and sellers trading freely. Private firms produce what people are willing and able to pay for.

ExampleRice and fish sold in the palengke.

Market failure

When the free market does not provide something society needs, or provides it badly, usually because it is not profitable.

ExampleNo private firm will build irrigation canals for small farmers in remote areas.

Natural monopoly

A service where one provider is cheaper than many, because the network is huge and costly to build. With no competition, that provider could overcharge.

ExampleOne water pipe network for a whole city; the country's seaports.

Public good

Something everyone benefits from and no one can be kept from using, so no private firm can charge for it.

ExampleFlood-control dikes, street lights, national defense.

The point: when the market fails, government steps in: through a regular agency, a regulation, or a government-owned corporation.

Reasons

Why do governments create GOCCs?

Fill market gaps

Why

Some services are needed but earn too little for private firms, so government provides them.

Example

NIA builds and runs irrigation systems for farmers.

Manage natural monopolies

Why

Where only one provider makes sense, public ownership keeps prices fair.

Example

The Philippine Ports Authority runs most public seaports.

Build the nation

Why

After colonial rule and WWII, new nations used state firms to start industries and keep key sectors in local hands.

Example

National Power Corp. (1936) built dams and power plants.

Earn money for the public

Why

Profitable GOCCs share earnings (dividends) with the Treasury, funding programs without new taxes.

Example

Landbank and PAGCOR dividends.

Move faster than an agency

Why

A corporation has its own board and budget, so it can hire, borrow and sign contracts more freely.

Example

Clark Development Corp. manages the Clark Freeport.

In short: a GOCC is used when government wants business-like work done for a public purpose.

Backstory · Before GOCCs

Before GOCCs: the State as merchant

1565 – 1898 · Spanish era

Royal monopolies

  • The Manila–Acapulco Galleon Trade (1565–1815) was a royal monopoly.
  • The Tobacco Monopoly (1782–1882), started under Gov.-Gen. Basco, forced farmers in Cagayan Valley and Ilocos to grow tobacco quotas.
  • It earned big revenue for Spain but bred resentment.
1900s – 1935 · American era

Building an economy

  • Philippine National Bank (1916) to give Filipinos credit.
  • Government buys the Manila Railroad (1917).
  • National Development Company (1919) to start industries.
1935 – 1965 · Commonwealth & postwar

Nation-building

  • National Power Corp., NARIC (rice) and GSIS, all 1936; later SSS (1957).
  • After WWII, GOCCs surged to rebuild the economy.
  • "Dismal financial performance" led to sell-offs in the mid-50s to early 60s (OECD).
1 · What people saw

The Crown's monopolies made money but forced farmers to plant quotas. Later, no private firm would build banks, railways or power plants for a poor colony.

2 · What they realized

Government business can build what the market won't, but power with no limits hurts people.

3 · So they concluded

Public enterprise is a tool: good for building a nation, harmful when used only to squeeze money from people.

Words to know

Money words: dividend, subsidy, privatization

Dividend

The share of profit a corporation pays its owners. For a GOCC, the owner is the government, so dividends go to the National Treasury.

ExampleUnder RA 7656, GOCCs must remit at least 50% of their annual net earnings.

Subsidy

Money the government gives to support an activity that cannot pay for itself, often to keep prices low for the public.

ExampleBudget support to a GOCC that sells a service below cost.

Privatization

Moving a government business or service to private owners or operators, fully or partly, usually to improve efficiency or raise money.

ExamplePhilippine Airlines (1992); Metro Manila water run by private concessionaires since 1997.

Direction of the money: dividends flow from GOCCs to government; subsidies flow from government to GOCCs. A healthy GOCC gives more than it takes.

Weigh it

Pros and cons of public enterprises

Advantages

  • Serves people the market ignoresNIA irrigation reaches farmers no private firm would serve.
  • Keeps essential prices fairA public owner of ports or water has less reason to overcharge.
  • Earns money for public programsGOCC dividends reached a record ₱138.5B in 2024.
  • Lets government steer key sectorsEnergy, transport and banking can be guided toward national goals.

Disadvantages

  • Politics in the boardroomBoard seats may go to political allies instead of experts.
  • Losses paid by taxpayersA losing GOCC needs subsidies from the national budget.
  • Abuse of perksBefore 2011, some boards gave themselves huge bonuses (MWSS).
  • Mixed goalsA GOCC must earn and serve at the same time; PAGCOR both runs and regulates casinos.
Words to know

Words to know: how a corporation is governed

Board of directors

The group that steers a corporation: it sets direction, approves budgets and picks top officers. RA 10149: "the governing body that exercises the corporate powers of a GOCC."

ExampleLandbank's Board of Directors.

Corporate governance

The rules and practices that make sure a corporation is run honestly and well, for its owners and everyone affected by it.

ExampleCodes of conduct, independent audits, publishing salaries.

Fiduciary duty

A legal duty to act in someone else's best interest, not your own. RA 10149 §19 calls GOCC directors and officers "fiduciaries of the State."

ExampleA director should not vote to give himself a bonus.

Oversight

Watching over an organization to check that it follows rules and performs well. The body that does it is often called a "watchdog."

ExampleCOA audits government accounts; the GCG monitors GOCCs.

A.1 · Governance Commission for GOCCs

The trigger: the 2010 bonus scandal

The problems had piled up for decades. In 2010, one scandal finally forced Congress to act.

July 2010

SONA exposé

President Aquino reveals MWSS officials got over 30 months of pay in a year through bonuses.

2010

Senate inquiry

Sen. Franklin Drilon's committees find similar perks in GSIS, SSS and other GOCCs.

June 6, 2011

New law

RA 10149, the GOCC Governance Act, is signed.

Result

GCG is born

A central watchdog for all GOCCs, attached to the Office of the President.

In simple words: GOCCs had no strict "parent," so some gave themselves huge bonuses. The GCG became that parent.

A.1 · Governance Commission for GOCCs

What exactly is the GCG?

The central watchdog

The central advisory, monitoring and oversight body for GOCCs, created by RA 10149 and attached to the Office of the President.

Business writers call it the "SEC for GOCCs."

5
members: a Chair (Cabinet rank), 2 Commissioners, and the DBM and DOF Secretaries
1 yr
term of appointive GOCC directors (§17), so poor performers are replaced fast

Current chairperson (2026): Marius Corpus, per Philstar.

Covered

  • Most GOCCs, e.g., Landbank, PAGCOR, PPA, PhilHealth
  • Government financial institutions

Not covered

  • Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas
  • Local water districts
  • Economic zones and research institutions
A.1 · Powers

What the GCG can do (1 of 2)

Evaluate & decide fate

What the law says

§5(a) Evaluate a GOCC's performance and relevance; recommend if it should be reorganized, merged, streamlined, abolished or privatized.

What it means

GCG checks if a GOCC is still needed and doing well. If not, it can recommend closing, merging or selling it.

Example

Northrail was ordered abolished in 2023 after 28 unproductive years.

Classify GOCCs

What the law says

§5(b) Group GOCCs into classes, such as developmental/social, commercial and financial.

What it means

A bank should not be judged the same way as an irrigation agency. Classes allow fair comparison.

Example

Landbank is judged as a bank; a social GOCC by the service it gives.

Set the rules

What the law says

§5(c) Adopt an Ownership and Operations Manual and a Code of Corporate Governance.

What it means

All GOCCs follow one rulebook on how boards behave, disclose and report.

Example

GCG approved its Code of Corporate Governance in April 2012.

A.1 · Powers

What the GCG can do (2 of 2)

Screen directors

What the law says

§15–17 GCG screens nominees and gives the President a shortlist; appointive directors serve one-year terms.

What it means

Not anyone can be appointed. Nominees must meet standards of education, experience and integrity (the "Fit and Proper Rule").

Example

Directors are reviewed yearly and can be replaced quickly.

Control pay

What the law says

Develop a Compensation and Position Classification System for GOCCs, approved by the President.

What it means

Boards can no longer decide their own salaries and bonuses. Pay follows one standard scale.

Example

Ends MWSS-style "bonus for everything" practices.

Grade performance

What the law says

Set up a Performance Evaluation System using yearly scorecards.

What it means

Each GOCC gets targets. Staff incentives are given only when targets are met.

Example

Only about a quarter of GOCCs passed in 2017 (Senate resolution).

Define it first

What is civil society?

In plain words

Civil society is the space where people freely form groups, outside government and outside business, to pursue shared interests or to help others.

Its groups are called civil society organizations (CSOs): NGOs, people's organizations, cooperatives, foundations, church and professional groups.

Think of it like this

In a barangay, the barangay hall is government and the stores are business. The parents' association, the youth group and the church ministry are civil society.

Four key features

  • Voluntary: people join by choice
  • Not for profit: earnings go back to the cause
  • Independent: not controlled by government
  • Organized: has members, leaders and goals
Formal definition

"The wide array of non-governmental and not-for-profit organizations that have a presence in public life, expressing the interests and values of their members or others." World Bank

Define it first

The three sectors of society

"Sector" simply means a part of society. Each sector is driven by something different.

1st

Government

Driven by: law and public authority
Funded by: taxes and fees

ExampleDSWD, your city hall

2nd

Business

Driven by: profit
Funded by: sales and investors

ExampleBanks, telcos, sari-sari stores

3rd

Voluntary sector

Driven by: shared values or causes
Funded by: members, donations, grants

ExampleRed Cross, a farmers' association

Formal definition

"Those sectors of Philippine society that organizes themselves into volunteers to take advocacy and action primarily for local and national development as well as international cooperation and understanding." RA 9418, Sec. 4(d)

Words to know

Kinds of civil society groups

NGO (non-government organization)

Formed by professionals, advocates or donors to help others or push a cause. Usually registered with the SEC as non-stock, non-profit.

ExampleGawad Kalinga; the Philippine Rural Reconstruction Movement.

PO (people's organization)

Formed by the affected people themselves to advance their own interests. The members are the ones who benefit.

ExampleA fisherfolk association; a tricycle operators' and drivers' association (TODA).

Cooperative

People who own a business together and share its benefits among members. Registered with the Cooperative Development Authority.

ExampleAn electric cooperative; a credit cooperative.

Foundation & volunteer group

Foundations give money or services from donated funds; volunteer groups give time and skills.

ExampleA corporate foundation; Red Cross volunteers.

Easy way to remember: NGOs help others; POs help themselves; co-ops do business together. All of them are CSOs.

Define it first

What is volunteerism?

In plain words

Volunteerism is giving your time, skills or resources for the public good, by choice, where pay or reward is not the main reason.

The person who does it is a volunteer. A volunteer service organization recruits, trains and sends volunteers to programs.

Think of it like this

Bayanihan: neighbors carry a family's house to its new spot, expecting nothing but a shared meal.

Examples you know

  • Red Cross blood donors and rescuers
  • Brigada Eskwela: parents cleaning schools before classes
  • Students tutoring children in their barangay
  • Doctors and lawyers giving free services
Formal definition

"An act involving a wide range of activities, including traditional forms of mutual aid ... for the attainment of the public good ... where monetary and other incentives or reward are not the primary motivating factors." RA 9418, Sec. 4(a)

Backstory · Worldwide

Why NGOs boomed worldwide

Step 1 · What was happening · 1800s–1940s

Charities did the work

  • Churches and charities ran most social help.
  • Red Cross (1863), Charity Organization Society (London, 1869), Hull House (Chicago, 1889).
Step 2 · The problem people saw · 1945–1980s

Pushed aside, then needed again

  • After WWII, welfare states took over services; voluntary groups moved to the side.
  • In the 1980s, debt crises and New Public Management shrank governments and cut services.
Step 3 · The idea that came out · 1980s–1990s

The "associational revolution"

  • Donors such as the World Bank, USAID and UN agencies channeled aid through NGOs.
  • The Cold War ended (1989) and a democracy wave freed citizen groups.
  • Lester Salamon (1994) called it a global "associational revolution."
So the conclusion was

NGOs rose because governments learned they could not do everything alone, and citizens' groups could reach people faster.

In simple words: when governments got too big, NGOs were pushed aside. When governments got too poor, NGOs were called back in.

Words to know

Words to know before pluralism

Power

The ability to make others do what you want, or to shape what gets decided. Robert Dahl (1957): A has power over B when A can get B to do something B would not otherwise do.

ExampleA mayor getting a road project approved.

Interest group

An organized group that tries to influence government to protect or advance its members' interests.

ExampleLabor unions, farmers' groups, business chambers, teachers' associations.

Elite

A small group at the top of society that holds a large share of wealth, status or power.

ExampleBig landowners, top business families, political dynasties.

Sovereignty

The supreme, final authority over a country. 1987 Constitution, Art. II §1: "Sovereignty resides in the people and all government authority emanates from them."

ExampleVoting in elections is the people using their sovereignty.

The big question of pluralism: is power held by one elite, or shared among many groups?

Backstory · B.1 · Before pluralism

Before pluralism: who holds power?

Step 1 · What was happening · 1600s–1800s

"Monism": one center

  • Power sits in one place: the sovereign State.
  • Hobbes, Leviathan (1651); John Austin (1832): law is the command of the sovereign.
Step 2 · The problem people saw · 1900s–1920s

Groups hold power too

  • British pluralists Harold Laski, J.N. Figgis and G.D.H. Cole: churches, unions and guilds also have authority.
  • In the US, Bentley (1908) studied politics as group activity.
Step 3 · The idea that came out · 1950s–60s

Explaining democracy

  • After WWII, scholars wanted to show how democracies differ from fascist and communist states, where one party held all power.
  • American pluralism (Truman, Dahl) became the leading view.
So the conclusion was

Pluralism came from watching how real democracies work: many groups share power, and no single master rules.

The "community power debate" that followed

Define it first

What is pluralism?

In plain words

Pluralism is the theory that, in a democracy, power is spread among many competing groups, not held by one person or one elite. Decisions come from these groups bargaining and compromising.

"Plural" means many. So pluralism means many centers of power, with government acting like a referee.

Think of it like this

A crowded basketball court: many teams compete, no single team owns the game, and government is the referee who keeps it fair.

Key thinkers

  • Madison (1787): many factions keep any one from dominating
  • Bentley (1908) & Truman (1951): politics is group activity
  • Dahl (1961): in New Haven, different groups won on different issues. He called real democracy "polyarchy": rule by many.
Formal definition

A theory of democracy in which public decisions result from competition and bargaining among many organized groups, with government acting as arbiter. Common textbook definition

B.1 · Principles

Six principles of pluralism, explained

Power is spread out

Meaning

No single group decides everything. Different groups win on different issues.

Example

Farmers may win on rice policy while business wins on tax policy.

Groups give people a voice

Meaning

Citizens gain influence by organizing into groups.

Example

One fisherman is easy to ignore; a fisherfolk federation is not.

Government as referee

Meaning

The State listens to all sides and sets fair rules, instead of serving only one group.

Example

Public hearings before a law or ordinance is passed.

Bargaining & compromise

Meaning

Policies come from give-and-take. Everyone gets something; nobody gets everything.

Example

Regional wage boards: labor, employers and government set the minimum wage together.

Countervailing power

Meaning

When one group grows too strong, others organize to balance it.

Example

Consumer groups forming to answer big companies.

Overlapping membership

Meaning

People belong to many groups at once, so no single conflict splits society completely.

Example

A teacher who is also a church member and a co-op member.

B.1 · Practice & critique

Pluralism in practice, and its critics

Seen in the Philippines

  • Party-list system (RA 7941): farmers, workers, women and youth can win House seats.
  • Local special bodies: NGOs and POs sit in development councils, health and school boards.
  • Public hearings before laws and ordinances pass.
  • Sectoral seats in bodies like the NDRRMC.

Criticisms

  • C. Wright Mills (The Power Elite, 1956): a small elite really rules.
  • Bachrach & Baratz (1962): the powerful keep some issues off the agenda.
  • Lindblom (1977): business holds a privileged position.
  • PH reality: dynasties and patronage tilt the playing field.
Words to know

Words to know: how CSOs get a seat at the table

Accreditation

Official recognition by the local council (sanggunian) that a CSO is real, active and qualified. Only accredited CSOs can sit in local special bodies.

ExampleA farmers' association submits its papers and is accredited by the Sangguniang Bayan.

Local special bodies (LSBs)

Councils and boards inside each LGU where officials and CSO representatives decide together on specific matters.

ExampleLocal Development Council, Local School Board, Local Health Board, Peace and Order Council.

Local Development Council

Prepares the LGU's development plan and investment program. NGOs must make up at least one-fourth of its members (RA 7160 §107).

ExampleThe municipal council that decides which roads and health centers to fund first.

People's Council

A body some LGUs create by ordinance to gather all accredited CSOs and choose their representatives.

ExampleThe Naga City People's Council.

What civil society does (1 of 2)

Service delivery

What it means

CSOs provide services directly, often where government is thin: housing, feeding, health missions, relief.

Why it matters

They reach remote or neglected people faster.

Example

Gawad Kalinga homes; Red Cross in typhoons.

Advocacy

What it means

Speaking up for a cause to change laws or policies: campaigns, petitions, lobbying and research.

Why it matters

Voices of farmers, women and PWDs reach lawmakers.

Example

Petitions led the SC to strike down the pork barrel (Belgica v. Ochoa, 2013).

Watchdog

What it means

Watching government to catch waste, fraud and abuse, then telling the public.

Why it matters

Adds checks besides COA and the Ombudsman.

Example

NAMFREL and PPCRV in elections.

B.2 · Roles

What civil society does (2 of 2)

Participation

What it means

CSOs sit in local special bodies and join planning and budget hearings.

Why it matters

Plans and budgets reflect what people actually need.

Example

A co-op representative voting in the municipal development council.

Bridge & mobilizer

What it means

CSOs explain government programs to communities and carry people's concerns back. In disasters, they gather volunteers fast.

Why it matters

Programs reach people who distrust or don't know government.

Example

Volunteers repacking relief after Typhoon Yolanda (2013).

Innovation

What it means

Small and flexible, CSOs can try new ideas and train communities and officials.

Why it matters

Good pilots get copied and scaled up by government.

Example

Community disaster drills later adopted by LGUs.

Weigh it

Pros and cons of working with the voluntary sector

Advantages

  • Close to the grassrootsThey live in the communities and know the real needs.
  • Flexible and low-costLess red tape; they use volunteers and donations.
  • Stronger democracyOrdinary people get an organized voice.
  • Extra checks on corruptionWatchdog groups expose waste and abuse.

Disadvantages

  • Fake NGOsShell groups can be used to steal public funds (the PDAF scam).
  • Uneven capacityMany lack money, staff or skills; some depend on foreign donors.
  • Unclear accountabilityCSO leaders are not elected by the public.
  • Political captureSome are created or controlled by politicians.

What it is — and why it exists

1The problemEvery office kept its own records, so one person walked from office to office.
2What that costsThe same requirements are asked again and again, and nothing moves until the queue does.
3The answerLink the records and put them on one app: one request, many offices. That shift is e-government and digital governance.
Words to know

Words to know: data, information, ICT

Data

Raw facts and figures that are not yet organized.

Example2,500 names and birthdays written in a barangay logbook.

Information

Data that has been organized so it means something and can guide a decision.

Example"There are 180 senior citizens in Purok 3," so the health team plans more home visits there.

ICT

Information and Communications Technology: computers, phones, internet and software. RA 12254 §5(l): "the totality of electronic means to access, create, collect, store" and share information.

ExampleLaptops, mobile apps, government websites.

Database

An organized digital collection of records that can be searched and updated quickly.

ExampleThe PSA civil registry; LTO's license records.

The chain: data → information → decision. ICT is the tool that moves government along this chain faster.

C.1 · Data & IT

How government uses data and IT

Five main uses, explained

  • Keeping recordsBirth, land and tax files move from paper to databases so they are not lost to fire or flood.
  • Targeting helpData shows who needs aid most, e.g., DSWD's Listahanan picks 4Ps households.
  • Mapping hazardsMaps and models show where floods may hit (DOST's Project NOAH).
  • Proving who you areA digital ID that any agency can check, so fewer documents are needed.
  • Showing the publicOpen data on budgets and contracts lets citizens check spending.

PhilSys national ID

RA 11055 (2018): one ID for all transactions. About 89.5 million Filipinos registered by Sept 2024 (PNA). It also helped millions of unbanked Filipinos open Landbank accounts.

Data Privacy Act

RA 10173 (2012) protects personal data held by government and business, and created the National Privacy Commission.

Freedom of Information

EO 2 (2016) lets citizens request records from executive agencies.

Words to know

Words to know: keeping data safe and connected

Data privacy

Your right to control how your personal information is collected, used and shared. "Personal information" is any information that can identify you (RA 10173 §3(g)).

ExampleYour name, address, birthday and ID numbers.

Cybersecurity

Protecting computers, networks and data from attacks such as hacking, viruses and ransomware (which locks files and demands payment).

ExampleStrong passwords, backups, security monitoring.

Interoperability

The ability of different systems to "talk" and share data. RA 12254 §5(o): to "communicate and exchange data in an accurate, effective, and consistent manner."

ExampleDFA checking your PSA birth certificate online.

Digital divide

The gap between people who have internet, devices and digital skills and those who don't.

ExampleFixed broadband reaches only about a third of Filipinos (UNESCO).

C.1 · Challenges

The challenges with public-sector data

Data silos

What it means

Each agency keeps its own database, and the databases cannot talk to one another.

Example

You hand the same birth certificate to the DFA, SSS, PhilHealth and your school.

Cybersecurity

What it means

Government holds millions of personal records, so hackers see it as a rich target.

Example

In 2023, a ransomware attack on PhilHealth leaked members' personal data online.

Data quality

What it means

Records can be outdated, listed twice or simply wrong, and decisions built on them go wrong too.

Example

An aid list still names families who moved away while newly poor families are missing.

Privacy vs usefulness

What it means

Sharing data between offices speeds up service, but too much sharing can become surveillance.

Example

One national ID linked to many agencies saves time; the Data Privacy Act (RA 10173) sets the limits.

A virus that no law could punish; millions of voters' records leaked.

2 · What they realized

Technology creates new harms that old laws did not cover.

3 · So they concluded

Digital government needs digital rules. In the Philippines, the rules usually came after the damage.

Define it first

What is e-government?

In plain words

E-government is government using ICT (computers, internet, apps) to deliver its services and information to people, businesses and other offices.

E-governance is wider: it also uses ICT to change how government works inside (its back office) and how it relates with citizens.

Think of it like this

E-government puts the counter window online. E-governance redesigns the whole office behind the window so it runs better.

Examples you know

  • Booking a passport appointment online (DFA)
  • Filing taxes with eBIRForms
  • Getting a business permit through eGovPH
  • Agencies sharing data so you submit fewer papers
Formal definition

E-government: "the use of ICT by the government to enhance access to and delivery of government services for an efficient, responsive, ethical, accountable, and transparent government." RA 12254, Sec. 5(i)

E-governance: "the use of ICT by the government to provide public services in a more friendly, convenient, affordable, efficient and transparent manner." Sec. 5(h)

C.2 · Weigh it

Pros and cons of e-government

Advantages

  • Faster, anytime serviceOpen 24/7, with no travel or long lines.
  • Fewer chances for corruptionLess face-to-face dealing means fewer bribes and fixers.
  • More transparencyEvery transaction leaves a digital record.
  • Better decisionsMore data for planning and disaster response.

Disadvantages

  • Digital divideFixed broadband reaches only about a third of Filipinos; elderly and rural people can be left out.
  • Privacy and hacking risksOne breach can expose millions (the COMELEC leak, 2016).
  • Outages and costeGovPH was down up to two days in April 2026; systems cost billions.
  • Bad processes stay badMoving a slow, paper-heavy process online just makes a slow online process.
Define it first

What are governance networks?

In plain words

A network is a group of organizations that work together on a shared problem, where no single one fully controls the others. They coordinate through agreements, trust and shared information, not just orders.

Intergovernmental networks link different levels of government (national, regional, provincial, city, barangay). Inter-sectoral networks link different sectors (government, business, civil society, schools).

Think of it like this

Old government is a pyramid: orders flow from the top. Network governance is a spider web: many points connected, each pulling its part.

Examples

  • Intergovernmental: national agencies and LGUs sharing one flood-control plan
  • Inter-sectoral: the NDRRMC, where agencies, NGOs and businesses plan for disasters together
  • Inter-sectoral: a city and a university mapping hazards together
Formal definition

R.A.W. Rhodes (1997) described governance as "governing without government": policy made through self-organizing networks of public, private and voluntary organizations. Rhodes, Understanding Governance

Words to know

Words to know: sharing power with local governments

Decentralization

Moving power and resources away from the national center. RA 7160 §2(a): "The process of decentralization shall proceed from the national government to the local government units." Forms: deconcentration, devolution, delegation.

Deconcentration

The national agency stays in charge but moves its own staff and decisions to regional or field offices.

ExampleDPWH regional offices and district engineering offices.

Devolution

National government hands powers and duties to LGUs themselves. RA 7160 §17(e): "the act by which the national government confers power and authority upon the various local government units to perform specific functions and responsibilities."

ExampleLGUs have run barangay health stations since 1992.

National Tax Allotment (NTA)

The LGUs' automatic share of national taxes (formerly called IRA). The Mandanas ruling (2018) said it must come from all national taxes, not only BIR collections.

ExampleA bigger NTA gave LGUs more money, and more duties.

Words to know

Words to know: public-private partnership

Public-private partnership (PPP)

A long-term deal where a private company finances, builds and/or runs a public project or service and shares the risks with government. RA 11966 §3(cc) covers "any public infrastructure or development projects and services" done this way.

ExampleToll expressways; airport upgrades.

Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT)

The private firm builds the project, operates it for a set number of years to recover its money, then transfers it to government.

ExamplePower plants built under the BOT Law in the 1990s.

Concession

The right given to a private firm to run a public service for a fixed period, under government rules.

ExampleManila Water and Maynilad running Metro Manila's water since 1997.

Implementing agency

The government office that signs and manages the PPP: a department, LGU, GOCC or state university (RA 11966 §3(q)).

ExampleDOTr for a railway project; a city for a public market.

C.3 · Philippines

Networks in the Philippines

Intergovernmental

  • Local Government Code (1991): health, agriculture and social services devolved to LGUs.
  • Mandanas-Garcia ruling (2018): LGU share must come from all national taxes. EO 138 (2021) ordered full devolution; EO 103 (2025) extended it to FY 2028.
  • Regional Development Councils: link national and local plans, with DEPDev (formerly NEDA, RA 12145) as secretariat.

Inter-sectoral

  • NDRRMC (RA 10121): government, CSOs and business plan disasters together.
  • Public-Private Partnerships: the PPP Code (RA 11966, 2023) lets private firms build or run public infrastructure.
  • Local special bodies: CSOs sit with officials (Part B).
  • eGovPH: one of 28 platforms DICT built with partner agencies.
C.3 · Weigh it

Pros and cons of networked governance

Advantages

  • Pools resourcesEach partner brings money, staff, data or skills.
  • Solves cross-border problemsFloods don't stop at city lines; networks can act across them.
  • Closer to the peopleLGUs and CSOs know local conditions.
  • Better-fitting policiesMore voices at the table mean fewer blind spots.

Disadvantages

  • Blurry accountabilityWhen many share a task, each can blame the others.
  • Slower decisionsMore partners mean more meetings and sign-offs.
  • Unequal capacitySome LGUs were not ready for full devolution.
  • Capture by the strongestBig business or powerful offices may dominate.
